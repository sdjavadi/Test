what the payment data shows

We rebuilt the payment extraction around the deposit book instead of around the payment graph. That single change lifted visibility of the corporate book from roughly a tenth to more than nine tenths, and it let us measure something nobody had measured before: how well the current attrition rule actually works.

It does not work well. But the same run also produced a rule that works substantially better, using only data we already have.

What changed: we can now see the book

The previous study concluded that payment data added nothing to deposit-based attrition prediction. That conclusion was explicitly conditional on coverage — only about 12% of corporate deposit customers appeared in the on-us payment graph at all. Anchoring the extraction on the deposit book instead reverses the problem.

The dollars moved further than the customer count. Payments where both sides bank with PNC are 6% of transactions and 18% of dollars — that was the entire previous view. The other 82% of dollars, including everything leaving the bank, is new.

Counterparty identification turned out to be a solved problem rather than an obstacle: 95.7% of external dollars carry a counterparty account key, a name, and the name of the counterparty's bank. Ninety-five percent of those keys are account-derived rather than name-derived, so a counterparty that disappears from a customer's payments is a real event and not a spelling change.

Settled

The current rule flags two of every five corporate customers

Applying the 30% rule across the panel, then its own exclusions — twelve months of history, a $25,000 floor, and a three-month persistence check — leaves 26,139 customers flagged, 39% of the book. Scored against customers whose balance series actually stops:

Share of book flagged39.0%
Precision — flags that were real departures11.3%
Recall — real departures caught61.3%
Lift over flagging at random1.57×

Departure base rate is 7.2%. A rule that flags 39% of the book to catch a 7% event is not selecting; it is nearly enumerating. Nine of every ten alerts reaching Treasury Management today are wrong.

A materially better rule is already available

Payment silence — three consecutive months with no payment activity of any kind — is by itself a better predictor than the balance rule. Requiring both signals is better still, and cuts alert volume to less than a third.

RuleFlaggedPrecisionRecallLift
Balance drop (current)39.0%11.3%61.3%1.57×

The trade is explicit and it favours the conjunction. Recall falls from 61% to 47% — we would miss a somewhat larger share of departures. In exchange the relationship team reviews 8,042 accounts instead of 26,139, and more than one in four of those reviews finds something real instead of one in nine.

Caveat carried forward: the silence label used here is "quiet at any point in the window", which is a blunt version of the signal. A proper as-of-month formulation should improve it further. This number is a floor, not a ceiling.

Promising, not yet scored

ACH origination is the tightest signal we found

The transaction data distinguishes ACH payments PNC originated from ACH payments a competitor originated for the same customer. That is a treasury service we either provide or lose, and it is measurable directly — no name matching, no inference. Across the book, 21% of outbound ACH dollars are originated by another bank.

A customer whose non-PNC origination share jumps more than 25 points over three months is a candidate alert. That fires on 3.7% of the book.

Two cautions before anyone builds on this. The level of non-PNC origination is a size proxy — 88% of customers have some, and small customers have structurally more — so only the change is usable. And we have not yet scored the 3.7% against actual departures. A tight alert rate is worthless if it is tight on the wrong customers.

Blocked — needs the deposit team

The deposit panel has had its departures removed

The panel appears to hold 30 months of history. It does not. Customers stop appearing and start disappearing in the same month, October 2025, and no real book behaves that way.

Two independent checks agree: every one of the 4,831 observable departures falls in the final eight months, and the panel carries a single as-of date rather than a rolling one.

Usable departure history is nine months and roughly 4,800 events — not thirty months. Every precision and recall figure in this document is computed on that window, and no analysis of earlier periods can be trusted.

This is workable but it constrains the design, and it may be recoverable. The question for the deposit team is what changed in the table's maintenance at October 2025, and whether pre-cutover departures still exist upstream.

Two things about the data worth knowing

Dollar thresholds have to be rail-specific

Wire is half of one percent of payments and well over half of the dollars. The median wire is $27,000; the median card payment is $65. Any rule with a dollar threshold in it behaves completely differently depending on which rail a customer uses, and rail mix correlates with industry and size.

Most counterparties are seen once, which limits one planned metric

Of 116 million external counterparties, 85% are seen by exactly one PNC customer and only 0.4% by five or more. Any metric that reads a counterparty's health from how several PNC customers treat it is undefined for almost the entire counterparty universe. That approach should be dropped or narrowed to the 0.4%.

The same sparsity shows up in relationship churn: a typical customer loses around 70 counterparties a month, carrying roughly 18% of the prior month's dollars. That is mostly one-off payees rotating, not relationships ending. Before counterparty loss can be a signal, recurring relationships have to be separated from one-time ones.

A cleaner switching signal, at a tenth the volume

Customers sending money to an entity with their own name — an account they hold at another bank — run at 6–7% of the book every month, steady. As a flag that is useless, because most corporates permanently bank in more than one place. A newly appearing same-name destination is an event, and it runs at roughly 300 customers a month, under 1%.

What we propose next

  1. Take the conjunction rule to Treasury Management operations. Balance drop plus payment silence, at 12% of the book and 28% precision. It uses data already in production and needs no model. Us, this month.
  2. Ask the deposit team what changed in October 2025, and whether departures before the cutover can be recovered. Until answered, all attrition work is restricted to a nine-month window. Escalation, this week.
  3. Request a monthly account-closure count, or per-account open and close dates. Today we infer departure from the balance series, which means we are predicting a deposit-derived event with deposit-derived features. A real closure date breaks that circularity and is worth more than any further metric work. Data request.
  4. Score the ACH origination shift against actual departures, the same way the three rules above were scored. If its precision holds at 3.7% volume, it replaces the balance rule rather than supplementing it. Us, two weeks.
  5. Rebuild payment silence as an as-of-month signal and re-score the conjunction. The current version is deliberately crude and the numbers above are a floor. Us, two weeks.
  6. Separate recurring counterparties from one-off ones before using relationship loss as a signal. The payment cadence work already scoped covers this. Next quarter.

How to read these numbers

Departure is measured as a balance series that stops and does not resume. It is a proxy. It is also affected by the panel problem above, so the precision figures will move when a real closure field arrives.

The rule is allowed to fire in any of 30 months while a departure can only be recorded in the last nine. That inflates the false-positive count, so 11.3% is pessimistic — the aligned figure will be higher. The comparison between rules is unaffected, since all three are scored identically.

Every figure here is descriptive. Nothing in this document establishes that a payment pattern causes a departure, only that the two occur together often enough to be worth acting on.
