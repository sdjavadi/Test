PNC Treasury Management, Data Science

Payment Knowledge Graph programme: deposit attrition early warning, versions 1 to 10d

22 September 2026. Internal.

The money leaves months before the account closes

Clients who end their deposit relationship with PNC have moved most of their balance six to eight months before the last account closes. A queue built on deposit balances notices after the money has gone. A queue built on payment behaviour reaches clients while much of it is still here: about $607 million of defendable balance in seven test months, which at a one-in-a-hundred save rate retains $10.4 million a year, eight times what the calls cost.

1What we found

The money leaves early
Between eight and six months before closure the balance index of departing clients falls from 0.86 to 0.35, while clients who stay hold flat above 1.0. One to two months out, when a closure-based queue calls, 82 to 91% of the relationship has already gone (§7.1).
Deposit balances see it too late
A queue ranked on a deposit-only model reaches the most departing clients at the highest precision, and 97% of them are already below a tenth of their normal balance; the average one still holds $579 (§7.5).
Payment behaviour sees it while it is still there
Scoring only client-months where the money is still on the book, weighting by relationship size, and crediting a save at no more than the client's normal balance, the queue reaches 229 departing clients holding $606.8m over seven test months at 1,000 alerts a month (§7.9).
It pays at the most pessimistic save rate tested
$10.4m a year retained at a 1% save rate, $9.1m after $1.3m of call cost. Break-even is 0.125%: one save in 800 conversations. The save rate itself has not been measured (§9).
Payments add value over deposits, reliably
Against a deposit-only queue of the same size the payment-informed queue retains $3.0m a year more at 1% (95% interval $1.7m to $4.3m); 45 particular clients would have to be absent to reverse it, and it wins six of seven months. Adding deposit features on top of payments is ahead but not reliably separated (§7.8).
The pool is larger than reported, and lumpier
Closures put $6.46bn a year at risk, not the $3.96bn stated in v9, whose annualisation divided by the wrong number of months. Clients who drain without closing add $5.19bn a year and 70% of that money never returns, but one client carries half of what a queue can reach there (§7.2, §7.12).

2The question

Treasury Management already watches deposit balances. The programme asked whether the payment graph (who a client pays, how, and how often) gives warning early enough for a relationship manager to act, and how much money such a warning could put in front of them.

Early versions optimised the usual thing: precision of a monthly list of names. Version 9 turned the question into dollars and found the binding constraint was not precision but timing. A list that is right about who will close is worth little if it arrives after the money has moved. Versions 10 to 10d rebuilt the evaluation around that: what a call could still defend, measured on data the model never saw, with the uncertainty stated.

Pipeline

  1. Customer-month panel, 2,746,664 rows: one row per client per month with deposit balance, live accounts and payment aggregates.
  2. Payment features, 1,952,288 rows by 59 columns: volumes and amounts by rail and direction, counterparty counts, internal versus external flows.
  3. Labels and peer anchors, 94,602 labelled clients and 120,457 peer rows: event months for every target (§4), and the peer groups used to normalise change.
  4. Risk set, 2,005,315 client-months still at risk of closure; widened in v8b to 345 columns with counterparty and institution features, and in v9 to 356 columns with balance-level features.
  5. Balance panel (v9): current balance, 12-month median, and peak for every client-month.
  6. Scored frames (v10c): every test client-month scored by every feature set on both risk sets, persisted so later analysis never refits.

How change is measured

Most features are expressed as change against the client's own recent past, then against peers, so a large client and a small one are comparable:

change = value(t) ÷ mean of value over t−6 … t−4
dd = change ÷ peer median of change, clipped to 0.01 … 100

Peer medians are cross-sectional within the same calendar month, so they exist at scoring time. Deciles are frozen from each client's first three months. Bounded ratios (shares, retention) use an additive change instead, because a ratio of ratios near zero is unstable. Missing ld_ features are set to 0 and missing md_ features to 1.

How the training sample is drawn

Attrition is rare (about 5% of client-months within six months), so training uses a case-control sample: every row that is a positive for any label being fitted is kept, and every other row is kept with probability 0.15. Each row carries its inclusion probability, and the fit weights each row by its inverse. That weighting was introduced in v10c after the earlier offset correction was shown to be wrong for this design (§8).

Time index

Months are indexed by an absolute m_idx (about 24289 to 24319), not from zero. Every month constant in the code is an offset from the first month; treating it as absolute silently produces no test folds.

6Methods

Model

A discrete-time hazard: one logistic regression per feature set predicting whether the event happens within six months of a client-month. Features are standardised and ridge-penalised (L2 = 2). Fitting is weighted by inverse inclusion probability and solved by damped Newton steps, which can only improve the objective; all 112 fits in v10c converged in 7 to 12 iterations with a final gradient below 10−9. Probabilities are then recalibrated with a weighted isotonic map fitted on the last two months of each training window.

Validation without leakage

Rolling origin: for each test month T, the model trains only on client-months up to T−6, so every training label has resolved before T begins, and is scored on the full, unsampled book at T. Seven test months, seven independent fits per feature set. Two checks run every time: a window assertion that no fold trains on an unresolved label, and a label-permutation control in which shuffled training labels must give chance performance on the untouched test month.

From a score to dollars

  1. Each month, score every at-risk client-month where the money is still here, and rank by p × normal balanceα.
  2. Call the top K. A client's first alert is its one conversation.
  3. If the client was genuinely leaving and the call succeeds, the balance freezes at the capped credit. If the client was not leaving, the call costs $250 and saves nothing.
  4. Retained = capped credit of reached departing clients × save rate. Cost = conversations × $250. Both annualised by 12 ÷ 7.

Stating the uncertainty

Comparisons between feature sets hold the queue decisions fixed and ask how much the result depends on which clients happened to leave. A client bootstrap (4,000 resamples) gives a 95% interval; a jackknife drops the clients with the largest contributions; a month-by-month view judges each test month as a standalone queue. α is compared at 0.5 and 1, fixed in advance: choosing α on the test months flatters whichever feature set has the noisiest curve.

Standing practice

From v10b on, each notebook states its predictions and a confidence for each before it runs, and scores them mechanically at the end. The record is in §8.

7Results

7.1The money leaves early

Figure 1 is the programme in one picture. Measured in dollars, departing clients hold 98% of their relationship a year out, 86% at eight months, and 35% at six. Two months carry 51 points of the decline. Defendable balance falls from $10.0bn at twelve months to $3.5bn at six and $0.9bn at one.

The money-move label D tries to predict that drop directly. Counted by clients it leads closure by a median of only two to three months, because small clients drain at the end; counted by dollars it leads by 5.5 months, because the clients who matter move early. In the top balance decile the median lead is five months and 47% move six or more months ahead.

7.2How much is at risk

The pool counts each departing client's normal balance twelve months before its event. A client can only be counted if a balance exists a year earlier, so events span 19 months, not the 31 v9 divided by. On the true span the annual flow from closures is $6.46bn, about 4.0% of the live book each year; clients who drain without closing add $5.19bn.

Corrected

v9 reported $3.96bn a year for closures. The correct figure is $6.46bn (+63%). Any share-of-pool statement built on the old number moves with it.

The pool is extremely concentrated. Its Gini coefficient is 0.93; the largest 1% of departing clients (101 names) hold 64.5% of the dollars and the largest ten hold 43.2%. The median departing relationship is $26k, the mean $1.0m. Any dollar figure here moves with a handful of names, which is why §7.8 reports intervals.

7.3Who will leave: discrimination

All four sets rank departing clients above stayers well at short horizons and less well further out. Combining deposits and payments helps at every horizon. On the money-move label D, payments alone are weak (0.57 to 0.62): payment behaviour catches a client during its move, not before it starts.

Read with §7.6

These AUCs include accounts that are already empty and only waiting to close. A model gets credit for predicting those, and deposit features earn much of their AUC that way. §7.6 removes them.

7.4Whether the probabilities can be multiplied by dollars

Expected dollars multiply a probability by a balance, so the scale of the probability has to be right, especially in the top decile where the queue works. It took three attempts. The fitted probabilities were twice too low; an unweighted recalibration then made them twice too high; a weighted one fixed the arithmetic but not the sampling design underneath. Weighting the fit itself by inverse inclusion probability brought every set to within about 6% of the realised rate.

7.5When each queue arrives

The direct test of the timing story. For the departing clients each queue actually reaches, what share of the relationship is left at the first call? A deposit-only queue ranked on probability calls clients who are already empty: 97% drained, $579 each. The payments-only queue calls fewer of them, earlier: 44% drained and $160k each. Adding deposit features to payments pulls the combined queue back toward the empty accounts, which is why at α = 0 it reaches less money than payments alone.

7.6Accuracy where it matters

Scored only where the money is still here, the deposit-only model loses 0.12 of AUC and payments lose 0.02. Retraining on those client-months, all features lead at 0.748, then deposits + payments 0.741, payments 0.731, deposits 0.627.

7.7What a save can retain

Crediting a save with the whole current balance rewards spikes: in 41.9% of departing client-months where the money is still here, the balance exceeds the client's normal balance. Capping the credit at the normal balance trims the headline by only 5.1%, so the earlier figures were substantially honest. But it reverses one earlier result. Ranking the queue on current rather than normal balance appeared to add 68%; with capped credit it loses 3.6% for all features and up to 23.6% for deposits only. Two clients explain most of the illusion: one held $306m at the alert against a normal balance of $1.0m, a one-off flow four months before it closed; another held 182 times its normal balance.

Corrected

The +68% from ranking on current balance (v10c §8e) was spikes being credited, not better selection. The queue ranks on normal balance.

Recommendation

Operate at α = 0.5. It reaches more money than α = 1 ($606.8m against $507.7m), 3.4 times as many departing clients, and depends less on its largest names. Both values were fixed before the test, so this is not a choice made on the test months.

7.10How many calls

Each extra alert reaches a little less money than the one before. The right volume is where one more conversation retains less than it costs. At a 1% save rate that point is 2,500 to 5,000 alerts a month depending on the set; at 10% every set is still worth calling at 5,000, the top of the tested range. The limit is relationship manager time, not economics.

7.11What earlier detection is worth

Take the clients the queue actually caught and ask what they held L months before their event. From two to six months the gain is modest, 1.2 times; the rise at nine months rests on about 64 clients and is probably a few large ones. For clients the queue already catches, catching them sooner buys little.

Revised

Earlier reads said lead time was worth more than all feature work combined. For the clients already caught it is not. The value of earlier detection is in reaching different clients: 38% of departing client-months are already empty when today's queue could see them, and the money-move target (§7.1) leads by five months for the largest clients.

7.12Clients who drain without closing

Counting B events as well as closures multiplied the dollars a queue reaches by eleven. That is not spikes: capped, it is still 11.0 times. Three findings explain it.

The dollars are almost all B. 86.5% of the attainable A-or-B balance, and 94.5% of what the queue reaches, belongs to clients whose balance fell below 5% of normal while the accounts stayed open.

One client carries half of it. The largest drained-but-open client held $3.04bn; it alone is 54% of the A-or-B queue's capped dollars. Without it the queue reaches about $2.57bn, five times the closure-only figure; without the largest ten, about $1.41bn.

Most of the money really leaves. Before quoting any A-or-B figure

Verify the largest drained-but-open client by hand. A $3bn balance that goes to zero with the accounts open could be a genuine exit, or a relationship moved to another customer key, legal entity or off-balance-sheet product. Half of the A-or-B dollars depend on which.

7.13Where the money goes

Over the last six months before closure, departing balances fell $13.04bn; net payment outflow explains $8.43bn of it (64.6%), and the rest leaves by routes the payment graph does not see. Six months out, 71.1% of departing clients' outflow goes to institutions they had not paid a year earlier. Transfers to accounts in the client's own name at other banks run 1.4 times the stayer rate six months out and 2.0 times at closure. These are flows, not balances, and cannot be added to the pool.

Restricted

Named destination institutions from v9 §5 are deliberately left out of this report. They are competitive intelligence derived from customer payment data and need a compliance read before they circulate.

9Limitations

  • The save rate is unmeasured. In back-test data a save and a client who was never going to leave look identical. Only a pilot with a holdout group can measure it. The case is argued from the break-even rate for that reason.
  • The freeze assumption is generous. Even capped, it assumes a successful call keeps the whole normal balance. Partial saves would lower every figure proportionally.
  • A handful of clients carry the dollars. Ten clients hold 45 to 75% of each queue's credited balance. Point estimates should not be quoted without their intervals.
  • Seven test months. Enough for the comparisons above, not for seasonality or a change in the rate environment.
  • About a fifth of the deposit book has no payment activity visible here and has not been profiled; for those clients only the deposit model applies.
  • Thresholds are choices, not estimates. Half the normal balance and $10k for money still here, $250 a call, 1,000 alerts a month. Sensible, and untested for sensitivity.
  • No live or shadow run yet. Calibration and ranking have been tested only on historical months.

Engineering traps

  • Month index is absolute; constants are offsets from the first month.
  • Keep Arrow off with PySpark 3.3 and numpy 2.
  • Bounded ratios need additive change; a minimum-reference gate removes them.
  • Use drop_duplicates(keep="first"), not groupby.first(), which mixes rows.
  • Isotonic weights and the intercept must use each row's true inclusion probability.
  • Fit logistic models with damped steps and check convergence on every fit.
  • An efficient frontier drops costlier points with no more money, not cheaper ones.
  • Display charts as image bytes when the backend is headless.
  • Do not name a DataFrame column mean.
